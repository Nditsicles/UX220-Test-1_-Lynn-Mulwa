From Physics to Global Web

The Original Research

In 1989, Tim Berners-Lee was a software engineer at CERN. Thousands of physicists from around the world worked there, and their documents, data and notes lived on different computers with different formats. His proposal described a way to link all of this information together so researchers could track, find and share it without having to learn each system.

The Web Today

Today the web is used by billions of people for almost everything: shopping, banking, streaming video, social networking, education, news, remote work and entertainment. It runs full applications in the browser, connects to smartphones and smart devices, and powers entire industries. What began as a tool for a small community of physicists is now a basic part of daily life.

What Stayed the Same

The core ideas have lasted. Pages are still written in HTML, located with URLs and delivered over HTTP, and the central idea of linking one document to another remains the heart of the web. The original goal of sharing knowledge across distance and across different computer systems is still what makes the web useful.

What Changed

The differences are mostly about scale and purpose. The first web served a few thousand scientists, mostly sharing text, while today it serves a global public sharing images, video, music and live interaction. Early pages were static documents, but modern sites are interactive and personalized. The web began as a non-commercial research tool, whereas now it supports advertising, online stores, large technology companies, in what amounts to a whole digital economy. This growth has also brought challenges that the original proposal never had to consider, such as privacy, misinformation and security.
